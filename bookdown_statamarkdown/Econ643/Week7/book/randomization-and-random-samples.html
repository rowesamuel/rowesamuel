Tests for one or two means

Chapter 1 Randomization and Random Samples

Is a sample statistic statistically higher or lower than a population mean? We need a random sample in this scenario. Acock (2023) uses the example of a small liberal art college about selectivity compared to the population. The mean score for a small liberal arts college for math and reading was \(\bar{x}=1180\) while the population average SAT score is \(\mu=1080\). Can the liberal arts school say that there selectivity is higher than the average college? We can test the example above with a one-group test for means or proportions.

Does a treatment has an impact on the treatment group? We need randomization into treatment and control in this scenario. Acock (2023) uses the example of a reading program for preschool children. There is a randomization of preschool students in and out of the reading program. On a outcome of 0-100 point scale, the children in the control group had an average score of \(\bar{y}=71.3\) with a standard deviation of \(sd=10.4\). In the treatment group, the average score was \(\bar{y}=82.5\) and \(sd=9.9\). It seems like the reading program had an impact, but we would need to test for statistical significant. We can use a two-group test for means or proportions for this example.

1.2 Randomization and random assignment

We covered taking a random sample from our data, but how do we randomly assign in Stata. There are many ways to implement random assignment, but we’ll cover a simple way to do so. Let’s say we have pre-SAT scores before treatment with \(\mu=1000\) and \(\sigma=50\). We randomly assign into two groups for a program to prepare for the SAT. We will use the following code with the function runiform() to randomly assign student and and out of treatment.

clear
set obs 100
set seed 101010
gen id = _n
gen preSAT = rnormal(1000,50)
sum preSAT, detail
gen random_num = runiform()
sort random_num
gen rank = _n
gen treatment = 1 if rank <= 50
list if treatment == 1

1.3 Significance level of \(p=0.05\)

I want to focus on Acock’s (2023) discussion of the significance level of \(p=0.05\). We test our null hypothesis, \(H_0\) with a one-tailed or two-tailed test. How do we know when to reject \(H_0\)? The rule of thumb for statistical analysis has been when \(p < 0.05\). We say that we reject the null hypothesis at the \(5\%\) level. Some researchers use \(p < 0.01\) to say that we reject the null hypothesis at the \(1\%\) level or \(p < 0.001\) to say that we reject the null hypothesis at the \(0.1\%\) level.

We may find \(p < 0.05\) just due to the fact that our \(N\) is extremely large. We may find statistical significance, but is it practical? We may find \(p < 0.05\) but our assumptions have been violated. (This is a preview for Econ 645 and Econ 672). Therefore, we risk rejecting a true null beyond our 5% level due to the bias. We may find \(p < 0.05\) if try to data mine and cherry pick results. Economist try to prevent cherry picking with extensive sensitivity and robustness tests. (Have you seen the length of online appendices?)

Here are some of my thoughts on \(p < 0.05\)

  1. Always have a healthy skepticism that your assumptions have been satisfied.
  2. Think about the arbitrariness of \(p=0.051\) and \(p=0.049\).
  3. Don’t cherry pick results. Run sensitivity tests to try to break your results.
