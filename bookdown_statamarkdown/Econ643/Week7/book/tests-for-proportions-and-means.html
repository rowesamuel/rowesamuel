Tests for one or two means

Chapter 2 Tests for proportions and means

We can use a one-sample test when we want to compare a sample mean to a population mean or a sample proportion to a population proportion. We can use a two-sample test of groups to compare two samples.

2.1 One-sample test of proportions

Acock compares adult opinions on school prayer from the 2006 General Social Survey. We will use the recode command to convert 2 into 0 and have a binary \([0,1]\) variable. We will use the tabulate command to make sure our recode worked.

cd "/Users/Sam/Desktop/Econ 643/Data/agis6r/"
use "gss2002_chapter7.dta", clear
recode prayer (1 = 1 Approve) (2 = 0 Disapprove), gen(schpray)
tabulate schpray prayer, missing

     Total |       341        518      1,906 |     2,765

Next we will test the null hypothesis that half of the people approve of school prayer.

\[ H_{0}: p=0.5 \]

\[ H_{A}: p \neq 0.5 \]

We will test this hypothesis with the prtest command with a two-tailed test. This will do a test of proportions for a one-group sample. The one-sample test of proportion compares a sample to a defined population mean.

prtest schpray == 0.5
One-sample test of proportion                   Number of obs      =       859

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Variable |       Mean   Std. err.                     [95% conf. interval]
-------------+----------------------------------------------------------------
     schpray |   .3969732   .0166937                      .3642542    .4296923
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    p = proportion(schpray)                                       z =  -6.0392
H0: p = 0.5

     Ha: p < 0.5                 Ha: p != 0.5                   Ha: p > 0.5
 Pr(Z < z) = 0.0000         Pr(|Z| > |z|) = 0.0000          Pr(Z > z) = 1.0000

Our sample mean is \(\bar{p} \approx 0.4\). We reject the null hypothesis that half of the population approves of school prayer at the \(0.1\%\) level.

2.2 Two-sample tests of proportions

We may want to compare proportions between two samples. Our prior example used SAT scores as an outcome of interest, where the treatment was a SAT preparation program. We saw that pre-treatment pre-SAT scores were similar between treatment and control. Now we want to compare the proportion of students who scored above \(1,200\).

Let’s look at our treatment and control again.

tab treatment
  treatment |      Freq.     Percent        Cum.
------------+-----------------------------------
          0 |         50       50.00       50.00
          1 |         50       50.00      100.00
------------+-----------------------------------
      Total |        100      100.00

We can check the null hypothesis that the proportion of treatment students, who scored higher than a \(1,200\), was the same proportion as control students.

\[ H_{0}: p_{treatment} - p_{control} = 0 \] \[ H_{A}: p_{treatment} - p_{control} \neq 0 \] Our data are in a long-format, so we will use the by option with the prtest command.

list treatment post1200 in 46/55
tabulate post1200 treatment
prtest post1200, by(treatment)

We see that a higher proportion of treatment student scored higher than \(1,200\). We reject the null hypothesis at the \(0.01\%\) level using a two-tailed test of proportions.

Please note that Acock (2023) uses an example of prtest when the data are in a wide format. You can review this, but your data should be organized in a long-format.

2.3 One-Sample test of means

We can use Stata to conduct \(t\)-tests for one-sample or two-sample tests of means. Our main command is ttest. For our one-sample test of means, we will compare the sample mean to a population mean. We hypothesize that the population mean of working hours for employed individuals is 40 or \(\mu=40\). Let’s find the sample mean of working hours from the 2006 General Social Survey.

cd "/Users/Sam/Desktop/Econ 643/Data/agis6r/"
use "gss2002_chapter7.dta", clear
summarize hrs1 if wrkstat==1
/Users/Sam/Desktop/Econ 643/Data/agis6r

    Variable |        Obs        Mean    Std. dev.       Min        Max
-------------+---------------------------------------------------------
        hrs1 |      1,419    45.97111    11.88977          4         89

We can see that \(\bar{x} = 46\) and \(s = 11.9\). Let’s test to see if this is statistically significant different from the hypothesis of a population mean of 40.

\[ H_{0}: \mu_{(hours)}=40 \]

\[ H_{A}: \mu_{(hours)} \neq 40 \]

ttest hrs1==40 if wrkstat==1
One-sample t test
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Variable |     Obs        Mean    Std. err.   Std. dev.   [95% conf. interval]
---------+--------------------------------------------------------------------
    hrs1 |   1,419    45.97111    .3156328    11.88977    45.35195    46.59026
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    mean = mean(hrs1)                                             t =  18.9179
H0: mean = 40                                    Degrees of freedom =     1418

    Ha: mean < 40               Ha: mean != 40                 Ha: mean > 40
 Pr(T < t) = 1.0000         Pr(|T| > |t|) = 0.0000          Pr(T > t) = 0.0000

We find that \(t=18.9179\) and \(p<0.001\). We reject the null hypothesis at the \(0.01\%\) level using a two-tailed \(t\)-test that the population mean. In addition, Stata provides a \(95\%\) confidence interval of \([45.3,46.6]\). We find that the average working hours is greater than our hypothesized 40 work week.

What is the problem? We don’t know the exact values. We only know the range of the values. Acock (2023) takes the midpoint, but this method is ripe with measurement error. Please see page 168 on how he recodes the variable.

Let’s compare hours instead.

\[ H_0: \mu_{(men)} - \mu_{(women)} = 0 \]

\[ H_A: \mu_{(men)} - \mu_{(women)} \neq 0 \]

ttest hrs if wrkstat==1, by(sex)

Our \(t=8.1802\) and \(p < 0.001\). We reject the null hypothesis at the \(0.01\%\) level and conclude that men and women do not work the same amount of hours.

2.5 Testing for unequal variance

We can test to see if the variance or standard deviations are similar between two groups. Why do we care if the variance are equal? Because we assume that the variance is the same between two samples from the population. We can use the sdtest in Stata to test for equal variance. This test uses an \(F\) test for significant differences in variances.

The \(F\) test is a ratio of the variances between the two groups.

\[ H_0: ratio=1 \]

\[ H_A: ratio \neq 1 \]

sdtest hrs if wrkstat==1, by(sex)
Variance ratio test
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Group |     Obs        Mean    Std. err.   Std. dev.   [95% conf. interval]
---------+--------------------------------------------------------------------
    male |     748    48.08422    .4630884    12.66528    47.17512    48.99333
  female |     671     43.6155    .4045276    10.47875     42.8212    44.40979
---------+--------------------------------------------------------------------
Combined |   1,419    45.97111    .3156328    11.88977    45.35195    46.59026
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    ratio = sd(male) / sd(female)                                 f =   1.4609
H0: ratio = 1                                    Degrees of freedom = 747, 670

    Ha: ratio < 1               Ha: ratio != 1                 Ha: ratio > 1
  Pr(F < f) = 1.0000         2*Pr(F > f) = 0.0000           Pr(F > f) = 0.0000

We reject the null hypothesis and the variance between the two samples is not the same. This will matter when we focus on Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). In regression, one of assumption is constant variance across values or homoskedasticity.
